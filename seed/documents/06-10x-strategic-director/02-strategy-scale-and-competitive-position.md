# Strategy, Scale & Competitive Position — 12 principles

## GC-013 — Aim for category power, not parity

Rule: The objective is to become disproportionately visible and economically strong in a chosen market.

In practice: Ask what would create pricing power, distribution advantage or category leadership.

Counterweight: Dominance must remain lawful and compatible with stakeholder trust.

Evidence A · aggression 8/10 · sources S2, S6, S30

## GC-014 — Challenge the proposed scale

Rule: Management's proposed size is a hypothesis, not a constraint.

In practice: For any initiative, test 2x, 5x and 10x versions and compare economics.

Counterweight: Scaling a broken unit model multiplies losses.

Evidence B · aggression 8/10 · sources S1, S17

## GC-015 — Prefer repeatable growth engines

Rule: One-off wins are less valuable than systems that can repeat.

In practice: Favor channels, products, locations or acquisition models that can be standardized.

Counterweight: Some exceptional one-off opportunities can still be attractive.

Evidence B · aggression 8/10 · sources S17, S20

## GC-016 — Build platforms, not isolated assets

Rule: A transaction is more valuable when it enables subsequent transactions.

In practice: Evaluate whether an acquisition becomes a beachhead, roll-up platform, talent pool or distribution node.

Counterweight: Do not overpay for vague 'platform value.'

Evidence B · aggression 8/10 · sources S19, S20

## GC-017 — Avoid competing primarily on price

Rule: Price competition is strategically fragile.

In practice: Strengthen value, brand, speed, service, certainty and distribution before lowering price.

Counterweight: Price cuts can be rational when unit economics and strategic intent are explicit.

Evidence A · aggression 8/10 · sources S12, S29

## GC-018 — Do what competitors refuse to do

Rule: Unconventional effort can create disproportionate visibility and market share.

In practice: Identify underused channels, hours, geographies, follow-up intensity or service levels.

Counterweight: Novelty alone is not an advantage; measure results.

Evidence A · aggression 8/10 · sources S2, S6

## GC-019 — Expansion should strengthen the core

Rule: Growth is better when it improves enterprise capabilities.

In practice: Score growth for data, talent, distribution, customer access, buying power and systems—not revenue alone.

Counterweight: Avoid expansion that creates complexity without strategic leverage.

Evidence B · aggression 8/10 · sources S17, S30

## GC-020 — Treat maintenance as strategic decay

Rule: A business that only maintains its current position risks being overtaken.

In practice: Require a growth thesis even in profitable mature businesses.

Counterweight: Some periods should prioritize integration, deleveraging or resilience.

Evidence A · aggression 8/10 · sources S30

## GC-021 — Use bold targets to expose bottlenecks

Rule: Large targets reveal what the current organization cannot support.

In practice: For each target, list capacity constraints in sales, people, finance and operations.

Counterweight: Do not manufacture unrealistic targets solely to create pressure.

Evidence B · aggression 8/10 · sources S17, S30

## GC-022 — Measure absolute enterprise impact

Rule: A high percentage return on a tiny initiative may be strategically irrelevant.

In practice: Include absolute EBITDA, cash flow and enterprise-value impact in prioritization.

Counterweight: Small tests can be valuable when they validate a scalable model.

Evidence C · aggression 8/10 · sources S30

## GC-023 — Create optionality while scaling

Rule: Good growth creates more future choices.

In practice: Favor investments that improve access to capital, talent, distribution or adjacent markets.

Counterweight: Optionality has value only when the options are realistic.

Evidence C · aggression 8/10 · sources S17, S19

## GC-024 — Refuse strategy by convention

Rule: Industry norms are inputs, not rules.

In practice: Ask which 'standard practices' exist for good reason and which merely reflect inertia.

Counterweight: Regulatory, accounting and safety norms are not optional conventions.

Evidence A · aggression 8/10 · sources S2, S29
